\chapter{Conditional Estimation and Generation}

In the previous chapters of this book, we have studied how to effectively and efficiently learn a representation for a variable $\x$ in the world with a distribution $p(\x)$ that has a low-dimensional support in a high-dimensional space. In this chapter and the next, we show how to apply these methods to learn the distribution and representation of many practical data which facilitate many real tasks in increasingly more general and challenging settings. 

In general, once we have learned the distribution of $\x$, it can be very useful for conducting subsequent inference in the world related to $\x$. As we will see, almost all practical applications can be converted to the problem that given an observation $\y$ that depends on $\x$, say
\begin{equation}
    \y = h(\x) + \boldsymbol{w},
\end{equation}
we need to solve the ``inverse problem'' of obtaining a good estimate $\hat \x(\y)$ for $\x$ or generating a sample $\hat{\x}$ that is consistent with the observation $\y \approx h(\hat{\x})$. To accomplish such tasks well, we essentially need to get a hold of the conditional distribution:
\begin{equation}
p(\x\mid \y) = \frac{p(\y\mid \x) p(\x) }{p(\y)}, 
\end{equation} 
where the last equation due to the Bayes rule.  Both the conditional estimation and conditional generation (or prediction) can be naturally inferred from the conditional distribution. 

In practice, however, initial information about the distributions of $\x$ and $\y$ and their relationship can be given in many different ways. In general, they can mainly be categorized into four cases, which are, conceptually, increasingly more challenging:
\begin{itemize}
\item {\em Case 1:} Both the distribution of $\x$ and the observation model $h(\cdot)$ is known, even in analytical form. This is typically the case for many classic signal processing problems, such as the sparse recovery problem we saw in Chapter 2 and the low-rank matrix recovery problem we will study below.
\item {\em Case 2:} The observation model $h(\cdot)$ is known, but we only have samples $\X = \{\x_1, \ldots, \x_N\}$ of $\x$. The distribution $p(\x)$ of $\x$ needs to be learned, and subsequently the conditional distribution $p(\x\mid \y)$. Natural image completion is one typical example for this class of problems.
\item {\em Case 3:} The observation model $h(\cdot)$ is known, but we only have the samples $\Y = \{\y_1, \ldots, \y_N\}$ of the observations $\y$. The distribution $p(\x)$ and $p(\x \mid \y)$ need to be learned from the estimated $\hat \x$ from $\y$. New view rendering from a sequence of calibrated or uncalibrated images is one such problem.
\item {\em Case 4:} We only have the paired samples: $(\X, \Y) = \{ (\x_1, \y_1), \ldots, (\x_N, \x_N) \}$ of the joint distribution of $(\x, \y)$. The distributions of $\x$ and $\y$ and their relationship $h(\cdot)$ need to be learned from the sampled data. Given many images and captions, learn to conduct text-conditioned image generation is one such a problem. 
\end{itemize}
In this chapter, we will discuss general approaches to learn the desired distributions and solve the associated conditional estimation or generation for these cases, typically in the context of a representative problem.
