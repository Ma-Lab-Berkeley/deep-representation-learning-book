In Chapter \ref{ch:linear-independent}, we have studied how to learn special classes of distributions whose support is assumed to be  low-dimensional linear or piecewise linear subspaces and different (linear) modes are independent. As we have shown, for such special distributions, one can derive rather simple and effective learning algorithms with good performance and efficiency guarantees. 

However, in practice, both linearity and independence are rather idealistic assumptions that distributions of real-world high-dimensional data rarely satisfy. Nevertheless, it remains reasonable to assume that the intrinsic dimension(s) of the support of the distribution is rather low-dimensional, compared to the dimension of the ambient space in which the data are embedded. 

Hence in this chapter, we show how to learn a general low-dim distribution in a high-dimensional space that is not necessarily (piecewise) linear or independent. It is typical that the distribution of real data often contain multiple modes, say corresponding to different classes of objects in the case of images. These modes might not be statistically independent and they may have different intrinsic dimensions. It is also typical that we do not know much analytical nature of the distribution in advance  but only have access to a finite number of samples of the distribution. 

To learn such a distribution, there are several fundamental questions we need to address: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item How do we even represent or approximately represent a rather general low-dimensional distribution? 
    \item How do we measure the complexity of such distributions so that we can effectively exploit their low-dimensionality? 
    \item How do we make the learning process  computationally tractable and even scalable, as the ambient dimension is high and the number of samples large?
\end{itemize} As we will see in this chapter, the fundamental idea of compression (or dimension reduction) that has been effective for the linear/independent case still serves as a general principle for the development of an effective computational framework for learning a general low-dimensional distribution.

Arguably of the most practical significance, we will study in greater depth how this general framework of distribution learning via compression can become especially explicit and efficient for an important family of distributions when the distribution of interest can be well modeled or approximated by a mixture of multiple low-dimensional Gaussians (or subspaces).
