Generally speaking, this manuscript is meant to systematically introduce mathematical principles and computational mechanisms behind how memory or knowledge can be developed from empirical observations. This is a fundamental capability for any intelligence, natural or man-made. We believe that the principles and mechanisms presented in this book are rather unifying and universal and are applicable to both animals and machines. 

We hope that this book can help the readers fully clarify the mystery around artificial deep neural networks by developing a rigorous understanding of their mathematical functions and roles (in learning data distributions). With such a clear understanding, we should have become fully aware of the limitations of existing AI models and systems: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Existing realizations of these functions are still rather primitive and certainly far from optimal in terms of optimization techniques or strategies. 
    \item Existing models and systems are far from complete in terms of a memory system that is capable of self-learning and self-improving.
    \item Existing models and methods are for developing a memory from empirical data, but are not yet for other human-level intelligence capabilities.
\end{enumerate} 

One of the goals of this book is for people to establish an objective understanding of current machine intelligence technologies and to realize what open challenges remain for the study of intelligence. In the last chapter of the book, we provide some of our views and anticipations about the future. 

\section{Towards Autonomous Intelligence: Close the Loop?}
From the practice of machine intelligence in the past decade, it has become clear that, if there were sufficient data and computational resources, one can build a large enough model and train it end-to-end to learn the distribution of all the data. Such a pre-trained large model can even demonstrate superhuman capabilities in terms of memorizing existing knowledge about the world\footnote{For example,  those knowledge already encoded in massive texts.}. In a way, such large models play a similar role as DNAs for the lives to pass on knowledge about the world.   

Nevertheless, as we also know now, such open-ended models themselves do not have the mechanisms to verify whether the knowledge they have memorized from the data are correct, nor have the ability to revise or improve them in case of being incorrect or incomplete. Improvements in these models depend on human experimentation, evaluation, and selection.\footnote{We may call this process as ``artificial selection,'' as opposed to the natural selection for the evolution of the lives.} 

As we have studied earlier in this book (Chapter \ref{ch:closed-loop} in particular), closed-loop systems have the ability to align their internal representations with the (sensed) observations of the external world and continue to improve. An immediate step forward for the future is to develop and build truly closed-loop memory systems that are capable of learning and improving acquired knowledge autonomously and continuously, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:open-to-closed}.

To a large extent, we believe that transition from open-loop end-to-end trained  models to closed-loop continuously learning systems:
\begin{equation}
   \mbox{\textbf{open-ended} models} \; \Longrightarrow \; 
   \mbox{\textbf{closed-loop} systems}
\end{equation}
is the key for machines to truly emulate how the (animal) brain creates and stores knowledge in an open world: 
\begin{quote}
\begin{center}
        {\em Open-ended models are for a closed world, however large; \\ closed-loop systems are for an open world, however small.}
\end{center}
\end{quote}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figs_chap8/open-to-closed.png}
    \caption{From open-ended deep networks to closed-loop systems.}
    \label{fig:open-to-closed}
\end{figure}

The practice of artificial intelligence in the past few years has led many to believe that one needs to build one single large model, either an open model or a closed-loop system, to memorize all knowledge. However, as we have learned from neuroscience, the cortex of our brain consists of tens of thousands of cortical columns. These columns are highly parallel and distributed, though sparsely interconnected. Hence, we believe that in order to develop a more scalable, interpretable, and modular memory system, we need to consider architectures that emulate that of the brain. Figure \ref{fig:loops} shows a massively distributed hierarchical system which consists of many largely parallel closed-loop autoencoding modules. These modules encode different sensing modalities or different aspects of each sensing modality. Higher-level autoencoding can be built on top of the lower levels to develop ``abstractions'' of the representations learned by the lower levels.

\section{Towards Intelligence of Nature: Beyond BP?}
As we have known from the practice of training deep networks, the only known scalable method to train such networks at scale is through back propagation (BP) \cite{Back-Prop}. Although BP has offered a way to correct errors via gradient signals propagated back through the whole model, it is nevertheless a method that differs significantly from how nature learns: BP is an option that nature cannot afford in terms of its high cost or simply cannot implement due to physical limitations. 

We cannot truly understand intelligence unless we also understand how it can be physically implemented efficiently. That is, one needs to address the computational  complexity of realizing mechanisms associated with achieving its objectives. Note that historically our understanding of (machine)  intelligence has gone through several phases in the past:
\begin{equation}
   \mbox{\textbf{incomputable}} \;
   \Longrightarrow \; \mbox{\textbf{computable}} \;
   \Longrightarrow \; \mbox{\textbf{tractable}} \; \Longrightarrow \; 
   \mbox{\textbf{scalable}}.
\end{equation}
To a large extent, the success of deep learning and back propagation is because they have enabled scalable implementation with modern computing platforms (such as GPUs). Nevertheless, such an implementation is still way too expensive for nature. 

We believe the future study of intelligence needs to facilitate a  following transition:
\begin{equation}
   \mbox{\textbf{scalable} solutions} \; \Longrightarrow \; 
   \mbox{\textbf{natural} solutions}.
\end{equation}
We need to discover new learning architectures and optimization mechanisms that enable learning under natural physical conditions and resource constraints, say, without conducting global optimization by BP. The new optimization strategies and mechanisms should work under conditions similar to those for intelligent beings in nature. They can be realized with significantly less resource requirements, amenable to continuous learning with localized and sparse update. These mechanisms may involve both backward and forward, continuous, local, distributed optimization. We believe that the hierarchical distributed closed-loop system architecture illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:loops} makes such learning mechanisms possible. 

\section{Towards Intelligence of Human: The Turing Test?}
As we have discussed in the Introduction (Chapter \ref{ch:intro}), the 1956 ``artificial intelligence'' (AI) program aim to study high-level functions such as abstractions,   logical inference, and problem solving that are believed to differentiate humans from animals: 
\begin{equation}
   \mbox{\textbf{low-level} (animal) intelligence} \; \Longrightarrow \; 
   \mbox{\textbf{high-level} (human) intelligence.}
\end{equation}
As we have clarified in this book, much of the technological advance in the past decades, carried out under the name ``AI'', is actually more closely related to the low-level intelligence such as memory forming, shared by both animals and humans. So far, there has been no evidence that suggests these mechanisms would suffice to achieve high-level intelligence that the historical AI program truly cares about. 

In fact, we know little about how to tell whether a system is truly capable of certain high-level intelligence, despite the fact Turing test has been proposed since 1950. To this end, we need to answer several scientific questions. First of all, how to systematically and objectively evaluate the level of intelligence for any given system? A scientific definition of the Turing test seems necessary. For example, how can we rigorously verify whether an intelligent system has truly grasped certain abstraction concepts, such as the notion of natural numbers, from only finite observed instances? Note that the state-of-the-art large language models still believe that ``{\em 3.11 is larger than 3.9}.'' How to verify a system that has either memorized or understood knowledge, or is truly capable of discovering new knowledge or discovering new causal relationships and mathematical abstractions. 

An outstanding question for future study is whether {\em compression alone} is sufficient to realize or achieve these high-level functions of human intelligence? Whether abstraction, causal, and logical reasoning are certain extreme forms of compression? Is there a fundamental difference between compressing empirical observations and abstracting certain high-level concepts or laws from empirical knowledge?

We believe that understanding the principles and mechanisms behind intelligence of different levels is the final frontier for science, mathematics, and computation.
